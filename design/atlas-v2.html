Atlas — v2 · four constellations

4 geometries · 6 real agents · 0 dependencies

In atlas-v1.html the constellation ended up as the alternative with one hard objection: it is the prettiest and the least responsive. This page goes after it alone and tries four different geometries to see whether that objection belongs to the idea or only to how it was drawn.

Everything that moves here is SVG, bézier math and requestAnimationFrame — not one library. The "app from the future" feeling doesn't come from effects: it comes from depth by layers (the background moves slower than the foreground), from light only under what moves, and from nothing arriving all at once. And above all from the fourth one, the expensive one: that the movement be data.

The question on this page is not which one looks coolest. It is: what does the movement mean? Every variant states it in one line, and two of the four fail on purpose — so the difference between a live thing and an animated thing can be seen.

The six agents are real (~/.claude/sessions, 24 Sep 2026). The messages between them are made up: these sessions have not talked to each other yet.

Try —

E1 · Chord ring pure topology

Every agent on a ring, grouped by project with a band on the edge. Each pair that has talked has a chord crossing the middle; its thickness is how many times. The names read radially, outwards. When a message goes out, a head of light runs along the chord and leaves it lit for a moment.

What the movement means: the head of light = a real message crossing, right now · the thickness = how many times they have talked · the node's pulse = it is working.
Decoration: none. Everything that moves here is caused by data.

Ring = everyone · band = project Chord = they have talked Thickness = how many times Light = message in flight

It is the best answer to "who talks to whom?" that exists in two dimensions. At a glance you see the knot, the sealed-off pairs and the one who talks to nobody.

Perfectly stable position. The spot on the ring is project and name: a session doesn't move from there all day.

It holds twenty. The ring grows in perimeter; the radial labels never collide because they fan out.

The middle is a knot. With heavy traffic the chords pile up exactly where there is nothing to look at, and you have to drop the opacity until they are barely visible.

There is no time. It says they talked, not when. A conversation from three hours ago weighs the same as one from a minute ago.

The ring wastes the middle: a 400 × 400 px square that can only carry lines.

E2 · Orbits — the clock of silence proposal

You are in the middle. Each ring is a project. And here is the idea: a body's angle is how long it has gone without doing anything. Twelve o'clock is "right now"; from there it turns clockwise as it goes quiet, and after ten minutes it stops at the upper left, in the dead zone. An agent that works comes back to twelve in a soft jump. An agent that gets stuck peels away from the group in front of your eyes, and you see that without reading anything.

What the movement means: turning clockwise = a while without touching anything · snapping back to the top = it just acted · the ring = the project · the arc between bodies = a message.
Decoration: none. There is no orbit for show: if nobody goes quiet, nothing turns. Hit "let them go quiet" and watch.

Top = active now Clockwise = time spent quiet Dead zone = more than 10 min Ring = project Center = you

The movement is the data. It is the only one of the four where staring at it tells you something you didn't know: who is falling behind.

It answers the question you actually have. It isn't "who talks to whom?", it is "has one of them got stuck without me noticing?". That is an angle.

The supervisor holds the axis, which is the decision the Room already made: you are not one more colleague, you are the center.

It reads best from a distance of the four: the shape of the whole — all bunched at the top, or scattered — reads from three meters away.

You have to learn to read it. "The angle is time spent quiet" is not obvious on day one. It needs the dead zone label always on, and probably a one-sentence tutorial.

Two agents from the same project that are equally quiet overlap. Here they are separated with a fixed radius offset, which is an honest patch but it is a patch.

One ring per project doesn't scale past six or seven before the radii get cramped.

E3 · Honeycomb control room

One hexagonal cell per agent, grouped into flowers of seven per project. The inner hexagon is the activity level; the border is the status. A message lights a path between two cells and leaves a ripple expanding out of the one that receives it, which is the cheapest way to say "something happened here" without writing it.

What the movement means: the inner hexagon growing = more activity · the lit path = a real message · the ripple = who received it.
Decoration: the flower's background glow, which is only grouping — it could be a line and would be just as informative.

Cell = one agent Flower = one project Fill = activity Border = status Ripple = just received

It scales best by a wide margin. The cells tessellate: forty agents fit without anything overlapping or shrinking.

Every cell is a real place to put things — name, counters, a mini-trace. The ring and the orbits only have a dot.

The regular grid scans very fast. The eye compares identical cells far better than loose points in space.

The topology is lost. Who talks to whom only exists while the path is lit; at rest the honeycomb says nothing about relationships.

The position inside the flower is arbitrary and looks like it means something. That is worse than meaning nothing.

It is the closest to today's grid of the four. Pretty, but if we end up here it's worth asking why we don't just fix the grid instead.

E4 · Stream the pretty trap

Between each pair that talks runs a river of particles, and the river's density is the conversation rate over the last half hour. A real message sends a bright bolus that overtakes the current. It is, by far, the one that looks best on video.

What the movement means: the river's density = messages per minute between those two · the bright bolus = a real message.
Decoration: the background particles, which are almost everything that moves. They flow constantly even when nothing is happening, and that is exactly what teaches a supervisor to stop looking at the screen.

River = one relationship Density = messages per minute Bolus = a real message

It is the most impressive, no argument, and it makes the system look like an organism instead of a list.

The flow rate is a real datum and it is one no other variant shows: the sustained rhythm of a relationship, not its isolated events.

It moves when nothing is happening. That is the cardinal sin of a monitoring screen: if there is always movement, movement stops being a signal.

It costs. Two hundred particles per frame against the forty shapes of the other three — on laptop battery you notice, and this screen is left open for hours.

You can't read a value. "Roughly dense" is not a quantity; nobody is going to tell four messages per minute from seven.

I include it so it can be ruled out with arguments, not because I recommend it. If you pick it, pick it knowing this.

Verdict — E2, with E1 underneath as a second layer

E2 (orbits) is the proposal, for one reason only: it is the only one where staring at it tells you something. The other three animate events — a message leaves, flashes and goes out —, so if you weren't in front of it, it didn't happen. E2 animates a continuous state: silence piles up as an angle, and an angle is still there when you get back from coffee. That is the difference between a screen that warns you and a screen that entertains you.

And it answers the right question along the way. With six agents across five projects, what wakes you up at night isn't "who talks to whom?" — it is "has one of them got stuck without me noticing?". That is exactly what E2 draws.

E1 (ring) doesn't compete with E2: it completes it. One is continuous state, the other is structure. The natural thing is for E1's chords to live inside E2's orbits — E2's middle is empty and E1's flaw was precisely wasting the middle. One single screen: rings outside, chords crossing inside.

E3 (honeycomb) is the honest plan B if this has to carry thirty agents, which is when E2 runs out of radii. E4 (stream) I would drop: it moves when nothing is happening, and a monitoring screen that always moves teaches you not to look at it.

None of this asks for new data. The rings and the angles come out of /api/activity (the timestamp of the last event per session, which already ships); the chords and the arcs, out of /api/meetings. The only thing missing is the same as in v1: an endpoint that returns all recent turns at once instead of one conversation per pair.
